Most gravity carts are built to a hobby standard: welded by hand, braked by whatever caliper was cheapest that quarter, and certified — if at all — after the fact. GravityCart started from a different brief, written by an automotive engineering team that happened to love the mountain: build the frame the way you'd build a chassis, then let the sport follow.

## Automotive tolerances, not workshop guesses

Every load-bearing part on GravityCart — the steering fork, the seat frame, the main chassis — is CNC-machined or cold-formed to tolerances of ±0.05mm to ±0.1mm. That is the discipline of a suspension arm, not a garden cart. It is also why the same mount point accepts a wheel assembly in summer and a ski assembly in winter without play, without a shim, and without a trip back to the workshop.

## Brakes borrowed from where braking is proven

The hydraulic disc brake on GravityCart is a Shimano MT system with a 180mm rotor — the same architecture trusted on downhill mountain bikes for over a decade. We did not prototype a new caliper to save cost. We specified the one already proven at speed, on gravel, in the rain.

## Certified, not just claimed

GravityCart carries CE certification and follows ISO quality processes through production. That is not a badge we added to the site; it is a file a notified body reviewed line by line — geometry, brake performance, structural load cases — before a single unit shipped.

## One frame, two seasons

The headline engineering result is the conversion itself: the wheel assembly detaches along its exploded axis and the ski assembly slides into the identical mount point in under five minutes, using ordinary tools. No proprietary fasteners, no factory return. Parts you can find at the hardware store, not the harbor.
