Search "descent-engineered cart" and you will find the term used loosely — usually by carts that were designed as a toy first and reinforced afterward. A descent-engineered cart is the opposite process: the chassis, the fork, the brakes and the seasonal conversion are specified together, from the start, against the actual loads of a downhill run. Here is what that looks like in practice, and what to check before you trust a frame with a real descent.

## Start with the chassis, not the paint job

The frame is where descent engineering either exists or doesn't. GravityCart's JM-001 is an automotive-engineered chassis — dimensioned to automotive tolerances rather than workshop guesses, so the geometry that keeps the cart tracking straight at speed doesn't shift from unit to unit. A chassis borrowed from automotive practice is built to hold a line under load; a welded hobby frame is built to hold together.

## The fork carries the steering loads

Steering geometry is where a fast descent punishes a weak part first. GravityCart's forged fork uses the same manufacturing discipline as automotive and motorcycle steering components — denser, more fatigue-resistant material at exactly the point where a gravity cart takes its steering loads run after run. This is not a place to accept a cast or stamped substitute.

## Braking is the whole point of the category

A cart with no motor has one job at the bottom of every run: stop, predictably, every time. GravityCart runs hydraulic disc brakes — the same braking architecture proven on downhill mountain bikes for a decade, not a prototype adopted for the spec sheet. Hydraulic actuation gives consistent lever feel and resistance to fade that a mechanical or drum system can't match on a long descent.

## Serviceable with tools you already own

A descent-engineered cart should not lock you into a factory return for routine upkeep. GravityCart's hardware — fasteners, bearings, brake consumables — is sized to parts you can find at the hardware store, not the harbor. That matters the first season you need a bearing or a bolt and don't want to wait on a proprietary part.

## One frame, built for every season

The clearest sign a cart was engineered for descent rather than assembled for a season is what happens when the season changes. GravityCart converts between its wheel assembly and ski assembly at the identical chassis mount point in under five minutes — one frame, one set of tolerances, carried through summer gravel and winter snow instead of two compromises sold as one product.

That is the checklist: a chassis built to automotive tolerances, a forged fork, hydraulic disc brakes proven at speed, parts you can actually service yourself, and a conversion system that makes the frame worth owning year-round.
